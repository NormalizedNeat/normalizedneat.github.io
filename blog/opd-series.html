OPD Series: On-Policy Distillation and Extensions in Post-Training

From OPD to OPSD, RLSD, and SDAR, this note traces how teacher signals move from direct targets to constraints, credit weights, and auxiliary gates in post-training.

At first glance, OPD looks like a technical variant of knowledge distillation. I find it more useful to read this line of work as a discussion about what authority a teacher signal should have during post-training. Across basic on-policy distillation, Rethinking OPD, Uni-OPD, OPSD, RLSD, and SDAR, the central question is not simply how to design another loss. The question is whether the teacher should define the target distribution, shape a reward, enforce an ordering, assign token-level credit, or only serve as an auxiliary gate.

The important object is often the same local gap: log pi_T - log pi_theta. Its meaning changes completely depending on the information structure. If the teacher and student see the same state, this gap may be a useful token-level preference. If the teacher sees a reference answer, retrieved skill, or other privileged information, the same gap may become a leakage channel. This is why the OPD family is better understood as teacher-signal governance than as a single distillation trick.

Reading lens

I organize the series by teacher authority. First, can the teacher give reliable next-token feedback on states that the student actually visits? Second, does that token-level signal agree with outcome-level correctness? Third, if the teacher uses privileged information, how much control should that signal have over the student policy?

1. KL Direction and the Basic Semantics of OPD

Classical distillation is usually off-policy. The teacher first generates a fixed set of answers, and the student is trained on those teacher-generated sequences through SFT or KL matching. This is cheap and intuitive, but it creates a structural mismatch: during training, the student sees teacher prefixes; during inference, the student must continue from its own prefixes. Once an early token deviates from the teacher trajectory, the later states may fall outside the region covered by the training data.

OPD changes this by making the student roll out first. Given a prompt x, we sample y ~ pi_theta(. | x) from the current policy. Then, for each student-generated prefix (x, y_<t), we query the teacher's next-token distribution. The teacher is no longer teaching only on its own comfortable trajectory; it is asked to provide local feedback on the states that the student really visits.

The objective is usually closer to reverse KL: 

L_OPD = E_{y ~ pi_theta(. | x)}
  [ sum_t D_KL(pi_theta(. | x, y_<t) || pi_T(. | x, y_<t)) ]

The KL direction is not just a mathematical detail. Forward KL places the expectation under the teacher and pushes the student to cover regions that the teacher considers plausible. Reverse KL places the expectation under the student and updates the policy only on regions that the student currently samples. In practice, this makes OPD a way to correct the student on its own on-policy state distribution.

If we look only at the sampled token, the local signal can be written as: 

r_t = log pi_T(y_t | x, y_<t) - log pi_theta(y_t | x, y_<t)

When the teacher supports the sampled token more than the student does, r_t is positive; otherwise it is negative. This is the main attraction of OPD compared with sparse outcome-reward RL. RLVR can tell the model whether the final answer is correct, but OPD tries to tell the model whether each token is locally reasonable under the teacher.

2. Basic OPD: Exposure Bias Is Reduced, but Teacher Reliability Is Not Guaranteed

Basic OPD has three appealing properties. First, it is on-policy, so the supervision happens on trajectories produced by the current student. Second, it is dense: every token position can receive teacher feedback. Third, it matches the autoregressive structure of language models because both the student and teacher are evaluated as next-token predictors under prefix states.

There are several implementation granularities. Full-vocabulary OPD computes KL over the entire vocabulary at each position, which is the most information-rich but also the most expensive because it requires B * T * |V| logits. Top-k OPD renormalizes over a subset of high-probability tokens, trading off fidelity and cost. Sampled-token OPD uses only the token actually sampled by the student. Even though it observes one token per position, its expectation is still tied to reverse KL because the token is sampled from the student distribution.

This last point is easy to underestimate. Sampled-token OPD is not a fixed one-point approximation over the whole training run. As the student policy changes, the sampled states and sampled tokens also change, so the method keeps probing the student high-probability region. The empirical story in Rethinking OPD is consistent with this: sampled-token OPD can be quite strong, while overly collapsed variants such as Top-1 OPD are much more fragile.

The weakness is also clear. The teacher distribution on a student-generated prefix is not automatically reliable. Early in training, the student may visit prefixes that the teacher would rarely generate. Such off-manifold states can make the teacher's next-token probabilities noisy. A high benchmark score does not imply that the teacher can provide stable, learnable, and task-aligned gradients on the local states visited by the student.

3. Rethinking OPD: A Strong Teacher Is Not Necessarily a Good Teacher

The most useful contribution of Rethinking On-Policy Distillation of Large Language Models is that it moves teacher quality away from a single benchmark number and toward thinking-pattern compatibility on student-visited states. A teacher should not only be globally strong; it should be able to teach the student at the states the student actually reaches.

The paper uses several dynamic indicators to explain successful and failing OPD runs. Top-k overlap ratio measures whether the student and teacher share high-probability candidate tokens under the same prefix. Overlap-token advantage measures whether the teacher provides useful probability differences inside that shared candidate set. Entropy gap measures whether their confidence levels are close. In successful runs, these indicators improve steadily; in failed runs, they stall or degrade.

The implication is that effective OPD is often not about pulling the student into entirely new modes from scratch. Instead, it redistributes probability mass inside regions where the student and teacher already have substantial overlap. One important observation is that overlap tokens carry the overwhelming majority of probability mass, roughly in the 97% to 99% range in the paper's analysis. OPD mainly adjusts relative preference in shared high-probability regions.

This also explains why a larger teacher can be worse. If the teacher's reasoning pattern is too far from the student's, the student's prefixes may look unnatural to the teacher, and the local target becomes hard to learn. If the teacher is too similar to the student, it may be compatible but provide little new capability. A useful OPD teacher sits between these extremes: compatible enough to be locally teachable, but different enough to contribute missing solution patterns.

Off-policy cold start and teacher-aligned prompts can both be read as ways to move the student into regions where the teacher can actually teach. Cold start does not reject on-policy distillation. It simply gives the student a better initial policy so that later on-policy rollouts land closer to the teacher's useful manifold.

4. Long-Horizon Limits: Dense Supervision Is Not Free

Dense token-level supervision is the main appeal of OPD, but it is not a free lunch. As responses become longer, student-generated prefixes can drift further away from the teacher's natural trajectories. The teacher may provide useful continuation probabilities near the beginning, yet become less reliable deep into a prefix that it would not have produced itself.

The response-length experiments in Rethinking OPD show a direct pattern. If the response is too short, there are not enough supervised tokens. At moderate length, dense supervision is valuable. When the response is pushed further, performance no longer improves reliably and the training dynamics can show overlap collapse, entropy spikes, or gradient-norm spikes.

The right panel is especially informative. If the teacher continues from a short student prefix, it can still improve accuracy. As the truncation length increases, the benefit of teacher continuation quickly declines. This suggests that the teacher is not uniformly reliable on arbitrary student states. The more severe the prefix drift, the noisier the local teacher judgment can become.

This matters for agentic RL. Agent trajectories are not only long texts; they include actions, observations, tool outputs, and state transitions. If the student takes a wrong action early, later observations change, and a teacher or retrieved skill may no longer apply cleanly. OPD in long-horizon agent settings therefore cannot be copied directly from single-turn math reasoning.

5. Uni-OPD: Token Gaps Need Outcome Calibration

Rethinking OPD asks whether the teacher signal is locally exploitable. Uni-OPD asks a further question: after token-level gaps are aggregated into a trajectory-level return, do they agree with final correctness? This is important because a teacher's local token preferences do not automatically induce the right ordering over whole trajectories.

For a trajectory, the token gap can be averaged into: 

G_OPD(q, tau) = (1 / |tau|) sum_t
  [log pi_T(o_t | q, o_<t) - log pi_theta(o_t | q, o_<t)]

If a verifier can distinguish correct and incorrect trajectories for the same prompt, a reliable distillation return should at least rank correct trajectories above incorrect ones. Otherwise, the teacher's token-level preference may look detailed but still prefer a wrong solution when viewed at the trajectory level.

Uni-OPD separates failure modes from two perspectives. The student perspective asks whether rollouts contain useful contrast; if all sampled trajectories for a prompt are correct or all are wrong, the learning signal is weak. The teacher perspective asks whether the token gap is trustworthy; a wrong trajectory may look stylistically teacher-like, while a correct but novel trajectory may receive a lower gap.

The core idea is order-consistency calibration. The verifier or outcome reward defines correct and incorrect sets. Then the OPD return is checked against that ordering and adjusted through masking or margin shifting when it violates the desired order. In this view, the verifier has higher authority than the local teacher gap.

This clarifies the relationship between OPD and RLVR. The teacher provides dense local preference, while the verifier provides global correctness ordering. When they agree, the OPD signal is useful. When they conflict, outcome-level correctness should constrain or override the teacher signal.

6. OPSD: A Natural Post-Training Idea and a Risk Entry Point

Once OPD enters RLVR or GRPO-style post-training, a natural idea appears. During training, we often have ground-truth answers, verifiers, successful trajectories, tool feedback, or retrieved skills. Can the model use this privileged information to provide dense token supervision for itself? This is the motivation behind OPSD, or on-policy self-distillation.

In a typical OPSD setup, the student samples a reasoning trajectory from the prompt x. The self-teacher is the same model, but its prompt additionally contains a reference answer or hint. On the tokens already generated by the student, we compute both student and teacher log-probabilities and use their gap as dense supervision. The appeal is obvious: the trajectory is still on-policy, no external larger teacher is needed at inference time, and token-level gaps can help with credit assignment where sparse RLVR rewards are too coarse.

Conceptually, OPSD tries to transform "the answer is known during training" into local explanatory power. An outcome reward says whether the whole chain ends correctly. The self-teacher tries to answer a finer question: given this prefix, which next tokens look more consistent with a path toward the correct answer?

The risk starts from the same design. The teacher is not only more informed because of model ability; it is more informed because it sees inputs that the student will not see at inference time. The teacher gap therefore mixes two components: transferable reasoning preference and conditional information from the reference. If the training objective asks the student to directly match the privileged teacher distribution, it is asking the student to imitate a distribution conditioned on invisible variables. 

P_S(y_t | x, y_<t) ~= P_T(y_t | x, r, y_<t)

Here r may be a reference answer, skill, or other privileged context. The student never sees r at inference time. A safer target would be closer to a teacher distribution marginalized over such information, not a conditional distribution for each privileged context. This is the information asymmetry that later motivates RLSD and SDAR.

7. Self-Distilled RLVR: The Problem Is Information Leakage

Self-Distilled RLVR gives an important diagnosis: naive OPSD does not merely overfit. Its objective treats a privileged teacher distribution as a target that the student must match. The teacher sees information that the student cannot see, but is still given direct authority over the student's output distribution.

From the KL decomposition perspective, the OPSD objective contains a term reflecting how much the teacher prediction depends on privileged information. The student cannot truly optimize away that dependence because the variable is absent from its input. Early in training, OPSD may appear useful because the student is far from the teacher marginal. Later, once the learnable shared component becomes smaller, sample-specific privileged detail can dominate the gradient.

This is privileged information leakage. The model may not simply memorize training samples; rather, the loss itself encourages it to imitate a teacher distribution that depends on information unavailable at inference. Reducing the support, for example with teacher top-1 or student top-1, can reduce the bandwidth of leakage, but it does not change the underlying information structure.

8. RLSD: Demoting the Teacher from Target to Credit Weight

RLSD's core correction can be summarized in one sentence: the privileged teacher should not decide what the student must generate; it should only help decide how much credit the student's already generated tokens receive. The RLVR or GRPO backbone remains responsible for the positive or negative direction of the trajectory update, while the teacher gap redistributes token-level credit inside that direction.

There are two branches to keep separate. The RLVR branch judges whether a trajectory is good or bad and produces a sequence-level advantage. The self-distillation branch compares teacher and student support on tokens that the student has already generated. The important design choice is that outcome reward decides direction, and the teacher gap decides magnitude. 

Delta_t = stop_gradient(
  log P_T(y_t) - log P_S(y_t)
)

w_t = exp(sign(A) * Delta_t)

Here A is the sequence-level advantage. If the trajectory is positive, tokens more supported by the teacher receive more credit. If the trajectory is negative, tokens less supported by the teacher receive more blame. The weight changes the scale of the update, not the sign of the advantage. That sign(A) is the key move: it demotes the privileged teacher from judge to attribution module.

Intuitively, exp(Delta_t) is an evidence ratio. If the teacher believes the token more strongly than the student, the token may be closer to reference-conditioned reasoning. If the teacher believes it less, it may be farther away. RLSD does not allow this ratio to decide policy direction by itself. It is only allowed to shape credit under the direction already determined by verifier or environment reward.

This closes the most dangerous channel in naive OPSD. The teacher cannot directly pull an unseen privileged token into the student support. It can only evaluate tokens that the student has actually produced. With clipping, teacher synchronization, and decay of the auxiliary signal, the authority of privileged information is limited to credit shaping rather than target definition.

9. SDAR: A More Conservative Interface for Multi-Step Agents

SDAR moves the same question into multi-step agent training. In this setting, teacher signals are even less stable than in single-turn RLVR because every action changes later observations. A retrieved skill can be correct in isolation, but if the student already took a wrong earlier action, the later state may no longer match the skill's assumptions.

The privileged information in agent tasks is also different. In math reasoning, the privileged input is often a reference answer or verified solution. In agent training, it may be a retrieved skill, historical experience, tool-use pattern, or task decomposition hint. Such information may be useful, but it is not guaranteed to be grounded in the student's current state.

SDAR therefore takes a more conservative route than RLSD. It keeps the GRPO objective intact and adds a gated auxiliary loss: 

Delta_t = log pi_T(y_t | s_t+) - log pi_theta(y_t | s_t)
g_t = sigmoid(beta * Delta_t)

L = L_GRPO + lambda_SDAR * L_SDAR

When the teacher supports the current token more strongly, the gate is larger and the auxiliary likelihood term is stronger. When the teacher does not support it, the token receives less auxiliary credit, but it is not directly punished by the teacher. This is asymmetric trust: positive endorsement is relatively useful, while a negative gap may come from skill noise, retrieval error, grounding failure, or state drift.

This is why the self-distilled component and the agentic RL component are separate. GRPO still learns the direction of task success from environment feedback. The skill-conditioned teacher only provides extra local reinforcement for actions the student already took. After training, the student should not depend on an external skill prompt; it should internalize the stable and transferable behavior patterns.

RLSD and SDAR both repair OPSD, but they do so in different regimes. RLSD fits RLVR settings where outcome rewards are relatively reliable and the teacher can shape token credit. SDAR fits skill-conditioned multi-step agents, where the safer design is to isolate teacher information inside a gated auxiliary signal. RLSD is about credit redistribution; SDAR is about risk isolation.

10. A Unified View: OPD Is Teacher-Authority Management

Across the series, the important trend is not "use a stronger teacher." The important trend is deciding what role the teacher is allowed to play. In basic OPD, the teacher is a token-level evaluator on states visited by the student. Rethinking OPD emphasizes that this evaluator must be compatible with the student's thinking pattern and must provide transferable information. Uni-OPD adds outcome-level calibration so that dense token gaps do not contradict correctness.

After OPSD, the focus shifts to privileged information. If the teacher sees a reference answer, retrieved skill, or any input that the student will not have during inference, direct distribution matching becomes dangerous. RLSD demotes the teacher from generation target to credit multiplier. SDAR goes further in multi-step agent settings and isolates the teacher signal as a gated auxiliary term.

The same quantity, log pi_T - log pi_theta, can therefore mean different things. It can be a token reward, a component of trajectory-level return, a leakage path, a credit weight, or an auxiliary gate. The right interpretation depends on what the teacher sees and what authority we give it in the optimization process.

11. Takeaways

My main takeaway is that OPD is not a simple story of asking a stronger model to teach a weaker one. The hard part is deciding when the teacher signal is trustworthy, how far that trust should go, and how the signal should enter training. A dense token-level preference can be useful, but it does not automatically agree with final correctness. If the teacher also sees information hidden from the student, direct distillation can import the wrong training objective into the student policy.

I now think of OPD as a problem of signal interface design. When teacher and student states are close, the teacher can provide direct local token correction. When token gaps may disagree with correctness, a verifier should calibrate them. When the teacher depends on privileged information, it should not directly decide what the student must generate; it is safer to use it as a credit weight or an auxiliary gate. This is why RLSD and SDAR are more interesting than naive OPSD: they reduce teacher authority exactly where information asymmetry becomes dangerous.
